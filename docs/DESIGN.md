# BoringSynth design notes

How the plug-in is put together, the rules it follows, and why the main decisions were made. Read this before changing anything structural. When a decision here changes, update this file in the same commit.

The step-by-step plan is in [ROADMAP.md](ROADMAP.md), and the maths each part calculates is in [MATH.md](MATH.md).

## How a note becomes sound

1. The DAW calls `BoringSynthProcessor::processBlock` with an empty buffer and the MIDI for that block.
2. JUCE's `Synthesiser` (our subclass `Synth`) splits the block at each MIDI event and assigns notes to voices. It handles note on/off, voice stealing, the pitch wheel and the sustain pedal. Before running the voices, `Synth` works out the LFO's effect for up to 512 samples at a time, into its `Modulation`, which every voice reads.
3. Each playing `SynthVoice` asks its `Oscillator` for one sample at a time, at the note's pitch moved by the pitch wheel and the LFO. The oscillator reads the current waveform's `MultiFrameWaveTable` at the note's Position, blending the two frames (each a `BandLimitedWaveTable`) either side, at the level whose harmonics all stay below Nyquist for the note's current pitch. The four basic waveforms have only one frame.
4. If the filter is on, the voice runs each sample through its own `Filter` (each voice has one, so key tracking, velocity and the filter envelope can give each note its own cutoff), its cutoff moved by the filter envelope and the LFO. Then it scales the sample by the note's level (velocity), the LFO's tremolo and its amp `Envelope`, and adds it to the buffer. The voice ends once the envelope's release has finished. If the voice was just stolen for a new note, it also plays the old note underneath, fading it out over 5 ms (its "fading note"). The output is mono, written to every channel.
5. The processor applies the smoothed Volume, then `protectOutput`: NaN or infinity becomes silence, and anything past full scale is clipped to it. This is always the very last step.

### Who owns what

- The **processor** owns the parameters (`AudioProcessorValueTreeState`) and the `Synth`, and shares the one `WaveformTables` with every other copy of the plug-in. The tables and the parameters are declared **before** the synth, so they outlive the voices (and the LFO) that read them.
- The **synth** owns the LFO and its `Modulation`, which the voices read.
- **Voices** hold references to the shared tables, the synth's `Modulation` and a `VoiceParameters` struct of references to the parameters' live values. A new parameter that voices need goes in `VoiceParameters`.
- **Tables are built once**, by the first copy of the plug-in to load, in its constructor, never on the audio thread. The last copy to go frees them.
- Each copy of the plug-in has its own **`WavetableImporter`**, holding the Imported waveform's table, declared before the synth for the same reason. It builds tables on its own thread, and frees old ones there too.

**Changing the saved state:**
- **Adding a parameter** needs no version bump. Older projects simply don't have it, and it loads at its default. The default should reproduce how the plug-in sounded before the parameter existed.
- **Renaming, removing or reinterpreting** anything saved means bumping `currentStateVersion` and converting older states when they load.
- **Loading ignores** empty data, garbage, and XML from anything else, leaving the parameters as they are.

A parameter's **range, display text and smoothing can change freely**, because projects store values in the parameter's own units, not knob positions.

## Real-time rules

The audio thread (everything called from `processBlock`) must never:

- **allocate or free memory**. No `new`, no growing containers. Voices and tables all exist before audio starts.
- **wait on a lock** that another thread might hold. JUCE's `Synthesiser` takes its own lock, but nothing else ever holds it, so it never waits.
- **touch files, the network, or the UI**.

Parameters reach the audio thread through `getRawParameterValue`'s lock-free atomics, read with `load()`.

Anything that needs real work (building tables, loading wavetable files) happens at load time or on a background thread. Results are handed to the audio thread without it waiting or allocating: see the decisions on the importer below.

## Behaviour the tests protect

- **New features keep the old sound at their defaults.** Each step is checked with the reference render (`Tests --record` / `--compare`).
- **Output doesn't depend on the host's block size.** The same MIDI gives identical output in blocks of 1, 7 or 512 samples.
- **Timing is sample-accurate**: `setMinimumRenderingSubdivisionSize (1)`.
- **Every waveform is zero at phase 0**, where notes start, so notes start without a click.
- **No aliasing from the waveforms' harmonics**: notes read band-limited tables, and anything at or above Nyquist is silent. (Reading between table points leaves errors around −118 dB, and the output's clipping can add its own when it clips.)
- **Levels**: each note is 0.1 at full scale. Eight notes peaking together stay below full scale, and the output guard stops anything louder from exceeding it.

## Decisions and why

| Decision | Why |
|----------|-----|
| JUCE coding standards, plus a blank line inside every namespace's braces | Matches JUCE's own code. The blank lines keep namespace braces from looking like class braces. |
| 16 voices, all created at load | Bounds the worst-case CPU no matter how much MIDI arrives, and never allocates. Notes past 16 steal a voice. A Polyphony parameter would choose how many of a preallocated maximum may play. |
| Volume −60 to +6 dB; the bottom is silence | A parameter needs finite ends. −60 dB is effectively inaudible in a mix and keeps knob resolution where it matters. The range or a skew can change later without breaking projects. |
| Volume smoothed **linearly in gain**, over 20 ms | Multiplicative (dB-style) smoothing can never reach a gain of exactly 0. |
| Velocity curve: (velocity / 127)² at 100 % sensitivity, blended toward 1 below that | The General MIDI / DLS curve. Velocity 127 is always full level, so turning sensitivity up only makes softer notes quieter. |
| Velocity sensitivity defaults to 0 % | Keeps the original "velocity ignored" sound. |
| Pitch bend range defaults to ±2 semitones | The common standard. This deliberately breaks the "default to off" rule, because a synth that ignores the wheel feels broken. |
| Bends glide over 10 ms, smoothed multiplicatively | Smooths coarse wheel steps while still feeling immediate. Multiplicative smoothing makes the glide even in pitch. |
| Sustain pedal on, sostenuto ignored | Sustain is on nearly every keyboard. Most synths ignore sostenuto, and JUCE's version also holds notes whose keys are already up. |
| Wave tables of 2048 samples, linear interpolation | Within 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ of a true sine (−118 dB), far below audibility, and cheap. |
| 11 band-limited levels an octave apart (1023, 512, … 1 harmonics), built with an FFT | Standard "mipmapped" wavetables. **Tradeoffs:** a note can lose up to its top octave of harmonics (the quietest ones), and bending across a level boundary changes brightness in a step. |
| A waveform is a `MultiFrameWaveTable`, a stack of `BandLimitedWaveTable`s, one per frame; the four basic waves have one frame each | One kind of table for everything the oscillator plays, so wavetables are just tables with more frames, with no separate path through the voice. Each frame is band-limited on its own, as each has its own harmonics. **Tradeoff:** each frame's 11 levels take about 90 KB, so a 64-frame table is about 6 MB; built-in tables stay at 64 frames or fewer. |
| Wavetables are added to Waveform's menu, with a separate Wavetable Position (0–100 %, default 0 %) | One choice of what to play, rather than a switch between waveforms and wavetables that adds a control and no sound. At a default of 0 %, older projects sound as they did: Position does nothing to a waveform of one frame. Greyed out on those, in the same way as LFO Rate while synced. |
| Position blends the two neighbouring frames, sample by sample, at one level; exactly on a frame it reads only that frame | So sweeps are smooth rather than stepping from frame to frame, and a single frame reads exactly as it did. Both frames read the level for the note's pitch, so a sweep doesn't change how far the harmonics reach, and moving Position never crosses a level boundary. |
| Position glides over 20 ms; each note starts at the current value; a stolen note fades at the value it had | As for the other continuous knobs: no zipper noise, and a note doesn't sweep in from an old setting. |
| Sine to Saw blends a sine and a saw in a straight line, across 8 frames matched to the sine's RMS | A straight line brightens across the whole knob on every note. Adding harmonics an octave at a time was considered, but the top half of the knob would then do nothing on most notes, as those harmonics are past the note's level. The frames are only needed for the loudness matching (a straight blend is about 0.5 dB low midway). |
| The built-in wavetables are worked out from formulas, each frame an odd function of the phase and matched to the sine's RMS | Nothing to license or install, and they're easy to explain and to test: each has a fingerprint of harmonics its name promises. Odd functions are zero at phase 0, so notes start without a click, and have no DC offset. Matching each frame keeps Position from changing the loudness: within 0.4 dB at every position, blends between frames included. |
| 8 to 32 frames per table, 168 in all, about 15 MB | As few as keep the blends between frames even: Hard Sync, Wavefold and Harmonics change fastest, so they have the most. At 90 KB a frame, 64 each would be about 46 MB for no audible gain. |
| Formant stops around harmonic 12, and Harmonics at 8 | A sound made of high harmonics loses them on high notes, as they pass Nyquist. Going higher would leave them nearly silent in ordinary playing ranges. |
| Every copy of the plug-in shares one `WaveformTables`, through `juce::SharedResourcePointer` | Building them takes about 0.4 s in Release and 2.7 s in Debug, on the message thread as the plug-in loads, and they never change. Sharing them means only the first copy waits, and a project of many copies holds 15 MB, not 15 MB each. It took the Debug tests from about 65 s to 27 s, as they keep one set for the whole run. |
| Imported tables are reference counted (`ImportedTable`), with the importer keeping a "release pool" of every one that might still be in use | The usual way to share something the audio thread reads and another thread replaces. Whoever holds a reference can read the table: a voice (the table it plays, the one it's fading from, and a stolen note's), the audio thread's current one, the window while it draws. The importer holds one too, so nobody else letting go is ever the last, and the audio thread never frees anything. It replaced a rule of step 32a, freeing a table after two blocks and 20,000 samples, which was only safe while every fade stayed short enough: a longer fade added later would have become a rare crash. Now nothing depends on how long anything takes. |
| The audio thread picks up a new table at the start of each block, from one atomic pointer, and records which | It never waits or allocates: taking a reference is an atomic add. The importer frees a table once it holds the only reference and the audio thread has picked up a newer one, so it can't take a new reference to it: both plain facts, not timing. A voice lets go of its tables when its note ends. Checked by breaking each rule, except one: the second fact only matters in a gap of a few instructions, a newer table arriving between the audio thread picking one up and taking its reference, which no test can line up on demand. |
| New tables are made one at a time, under a lock of their own | A file can finish loading on the importer's thread just as a project loads on the message thread. One at a time, they're numbered and reach the audio thread in the order they were made. Neither lock is ever taken on the audio thread. |
| A file of whole 2048-sample frames is split into frames, up to 256; anything else up to 8192 samples is one cycle; anything else is refused | 2048 is the usual frame length, and a clear refusal beats playing a song as a wavetable. 256 frames take about 23 MB. |
| Each imported frame has its DC offset removed and is matched to the sine's RMS, its peak at most 3 times the sine's | So Position changes the tone rather than the loudness, as with the built-in tables, and a file's overall level doesn't matter. The cap keeps a spiky frame from being scaled up until it clips; a silent frame stays silent. |
| A note on a table that doesn't start at zero fades in over 5 ms, before the filter | Imported frames can start anywhere, and a note starting at full level there would click. A table works out whether it needs it from its frames, at every level, so the built-in tables have no fade and stay bit-for-bit as they were. It's the shortest release's length, the amp envelope's click-free slope. Before the filter, so the filter sees no jump either. |
| An imported table is embedded in projects and presets, as its frames after processing | So moving or deleting the file can't break a project or preset, and it sounds the same on another computer. The processed frames are what the table is built from, so it comes back exactly, bit for bit, with no file reading or processing, and no dependence on the file's format. About 2.8 MB at 256 frames, the most a file can have. Standard base64, which records no length of its own, so a copy cut short is caught: JUCE's own base64 records its length and fills in what's missing with zeros. No state version bump, as it's an addition: older states simply have no table. |
| A saved table is rebuilt before the project or preset finishes loading, not on the importer's thread | A host can start rendering straight after loading a project (an export, say), which would otherwise play a sine until the table was ready. It takes about half a second for 256 frames in Release. It's handed to the audio thread in the same way as one from a file. |
| A project without a table unloads the one loaded; a preset without one, or a factory preset, keeps it | A project is everything about the plug-in, so opening it should give exactly what was saved. A preset is a sound to try out: trying presets on a table you've just loaded shouldn't lose it. A preset saved with a table brings it with it. |
| A saved table that can't be read is refused, with the reason beside the Load wavetable button, and the rest still loads | A damaged project should still open, as everything else in it is fine. Waveform stays on Imported, playing a sine, so loading the file again puts it right. |
| Loading is from a button in the preset bar, and switches to Imported once it's loaded | The window stays the same size. Switching only on success leaves the sound alone when a file won't load. |
| The oscillator remembers its level until the pitch changes | Step 28b first made every note about a third slower, blending or not (16 sines: 2.95 % to 4.0 % of a core), and why isn't fully understood. Not searching for the level on every sample more than made up for it: a held note is at about 2.7 %, below where it started, and blending at about 3 %. |
| Waveforms matched to the sine's RMS | Simple, explainable loudness matching. Triangle and saw are scaled by √1.5, square by √0.5, sine by exactly 1, so the sine stays bit-for-bit unchanged. |
| Waveform changes during a note crossfade over 10 ms, counted in whole samples | Avoids a click at the switch. Whole samples make the fade end on exactly its last sample, which floats didn't. |
| Our own `Envelope` class rather than `juce::ADSR` | `juce::ADSR` jumps when the sustain level changes mid-note. Updating its settings every block (needed so knob moves reach held notes) also resets the release speed from the sustain level, so a note released early fades too fast, and with a sustain of 0 it cuts off instantly. Ours glides sustain changes over 20 ms, always releases from the note's current level over the release time, and takes settings every block. Each case has a test. |
| Envelope defaults: attack 0, sustain 100 %, release 5 ms (decay 200 ms) | Reproduces the old fixed 5 ms fade bit for bit. An instant attack is click-free because every waveform starts at zero. Decay only matters once sustain is lowered. |
| Decay and release can't go below 5 ms | So the envelope can never jump in level and click. |
| Envelope parameter IDs start with `amp` | A filter envelope with its own attack, decay, sustain and release comes in step 17, and IDs can't be renamed later. |
| Time knobs 0 or 5 ms up to 10 s, skewed so the middle is 0.5 s | Most of the knob's travel goes to short times, where small changes matter most. |
| Tail length reported to the DAW is the release time | So the DAW keeps processing until notes have finished fading out. |
| The editor (`BoringSynthEditor`) is plain: JUCE's standard knobs, menus and tick boxes in its default look, in seven labelled groups on a fixed grid, with narrower rows spread to the full width | In keeping with the name, and every control behaves as JUCE users expect. Spreading a row shares its spare width among its cells; each control keeps its own size, centred. Step 24 used JUCE's generic editor, one long list; this groups the controls the way the sound flows. The layout is a table in `BoringSynthEditor.cpp` (group, parameter, label); a parameter's kind decides its control. |
| Controls are connected with JUCE's parameter attachments | They keep control and parameter in step both ways, with host automation, and give knobs the parameter's range, skew, value text and double-click default. A knob adds the parameter's separate unit label ("%", "dB") after its value text. The editor only reads and writes parameters, so it can't change the sound, and is checked not to. |
| The window resizes by scaling everything, 75 % to 200 %, keeping its shape | The layout stays the one tested grid, just drawn bigger or smaller: each component is laid out at the normal size and given the same zoom transform, so text and knobs stay sharp. A layout that reflowed at each size would need its own design and tests at every size. The components stay the window's direct children, so tests find them by parameter ID as before. |
| The window's size is saved with the project as `editorScale`, not as a parameter, and not in presets | It's how you like the window, not part of the sound: a host shouldn't automate it, and loading a preset shouldn't resize the window. The processor keeps it, so the window also reopens at the same size within a session. Restoring a project sets the size for the next window opened; an open one keeps its size. |
| High-DPI needs nothing of our own | JUCE draws knobs, text and outlines as shapes, at the display's pixel density, so a 200 % display gets a sharp window. Checked with `Tests --snapshot <file> 2`. |
| No envelope or filter displays | The values are already shown under each knob, and drawing them is the most code for the least "boring". They'd fit alongside the wavetable display, which needs the same kind of drawing. |
| The wavetable display (`WavetableDisplay`) is a small picture under the Waveform menu: one cycle, a line for silence, and on a wavetable a marker for the position | Under the menu it takes no room of its own, and the window stays the same size. A number can't show what a frame looks like, as a knob's value can show an envelope time. It draws the richest level, so it shows the waveform rather than what a high note has left of it. It's scaled to the table's loudest sample, so moving through a table doesn't change the scale. |
| It follows the newest note's real Position, or the knob with no note playing | Otherwise nothing would move during a sweep by the envelope or the LFO, which is when it's most worth watching. Notes on the same knob and LFO differ only by their envelopes, and the newest is the one just played. |
| The processor publishes the newest note's Position as one atomic float, after each block | No locks, and nothing that touches the sound: it's read from the voices once the block is rendered. It keeps its own list of voices, as JUCE's `getVoice` takes the synth's lock. The window reads it 30 times a second, and redraws only when something's changed. |
| LFO Rate is greyed out while LFO Sync is on, and LFO Sync Rate while it's off | Only one sets the LFO's speed at a time. Done with a `ParameterAttachment` on LFO Sync, so it follows automation too, on the message thread. |
| Each control's component ID is its parameter's ID | So tests can find every control and check it's the right kind and connected. A test fails if any parameter has no control, or two. |
| Preset files are the plug-in's saved state, as XML | One format for projects and presets, readable and diffable. Loading one sets every parameter: those it holds from the file, any it doesn't (a preset older than the parameter) to their defaults, as an older project's would. A file that isn't this plug-in's state, or is from a newer version, is refused, changing nothing. Each parameter is set as one host gesture, so hosts recording automation see clean changes. |
| Your presets live in `Documents/BoringSynth/Presets`, as one file each, with no browser beyond the menu | Easy to find, back up and share. The folder is only made when you first save. The menu re-reads it whenever it opens, so renaming or deleting files there is how presets are managed. Names are made into legal file names ("Bass/Lead: v2?" saves as "BassLead v2"). Files are only touched on the message thread. |
| Factory presets are built in, as a table of the parameters each sets (everything else at its default), with Init first | Nothing to install or lose. Init setting nothing guarantees it's exactly the plug-in's first sound (a test renders it). Each of the others shows off a feature. |
| The factory presets are also the host's programs; choosing the program that's already current does nothing | So DAWs' own program menus list them. Some hosts set the program again when reopening a project, after restoring its settings; reloading the preset then would throw away changes made since choosing it. The cost: choosing the current program again from the host doesn't reset it (the plug-in's own menu does). |
| One more program after the factory presets, `(User preset)`, stands for anything else, and choosing it does nothing | When the current preset isn't a factory one, the host needs a current program to see. It used to see Init's, but then choosing Init from the host after loading one of your presets looked like choosing the current program, and did nothing. A program of its own makes the two distinguishable: re-setting it on reopening keeps your settings, and choosing Init loads Init. Found by a code review. |
| Restoring a state sets each on/off parameter to exactly 0 or 1 | JUCE's on/off parameters keep any value a host gives them (0.22 counts as off), and restoring a state skips a value that already counts as equal, so a host could read back 0.22 rather than the 0 it saved. pluginval caught this intermittently. |
| The project remembers the current preset's name | So it reopens showing the preset it was on. Projects from before presets show no name. |
| A stolen voice fades its old note out over 5 ms, underneath the new note | JUCE's synth stops a stolen voice with no tail-off, and stopping a sounding note dead clicks. The voice keeps a snapshot of the old note (oscillator, pitch, level) in a slot that's part of the voice, so nothing is allocated, and fades it for the shortest release time. It has four such slots, so a voice stolen again before an earlier fade has finished keeps fading that one too (it used to have one, and the newer fade replaced the older, cutting it off: found by a code review). Filling all four takes five steals of one voice within 5 ms; the next then replaces the quietest. Stealing is the only way a note gets cut off; JUCE handles MIDI "all sound off" with a normal release. |
| A filter on every voice, not one on the output | With identical settings, one filter on the mix would sound the same, since the filter is linear. But key tracking, the filter envelope and velocity → filter (steps 16–18) give each note its own cutoff, and each note should start with a clean filter rather than one still ringing from earlier notes. One filter shared by all voices is called "paraphonic": a distinct sound, but usually a hardware compromise. |
| The filter has an on/off switch, off by default | A low-pass filter even fully open shifts the phase of high frequencies, so "always on, cutoff at 20 kHz" wouldn't reproduce the old sound exactly. Older projects load with it off, bit-for-bit unchanged. Serum and Vital also start with their filters off. Switching it during a note crossfades over 10 ms. |
| Our own `Filter` class rather than `juce::dsp::StateVariableTPTFilter` | The same design (Simper's TPT state-variable filter, stable at any setting), but as a few plain numbers. JUCE's keeps its state in `std::vector`s, so copying it allocates, and a stolen voice's fading note copies its filter on the audio thread. It also gives band-pass and high-pass for step 15. |
| Filter types: low-pass (default), high-pass, band-pass, all from one calculation, each with a gain of Q at the cutoff | The state-variable filter produces all three at once, so offering them costs almost nothing. With the same gain at the cutoff, Resonance behaves the same for every type, and switching types doesn't jump in level there. The band-pass is left unnormalised (its peak rises with resonance), matching the other two. |
| A filter type change crossfades over 10 ms by blending the filter's outputs | At the cutoff, low-pass and high-pass are half a cycle apart, so an instant switch jumps by up to twice the note's level. Each voice keeps a smoothed "amount" per type and blends the three outputs; with one type chosen it's exactly that type's output (`1 × it + 0 × the others`), so nothing changes when the type isn't moving. No second filter is needed. |
| Signal order: oscillator → filter → amp envelope | The standard subtractive synth order. The filter is linear, so this only matters once later steps make the filter's settings depend on the note. |
| Resonance 0–100 % maps to Q 0.707–10, evenly on a log scale | 0 % is flat (no peak); 100 % is a +20 dB peak. This filter can't self-oscillate at any finite Q, so there's no runaway. |
| Cutoff 20 Hz – 20 kHz, knob centre about 632 Hz | The range of hearing, skewed so equal turns are equal steps in pitch. It's capped just below Nyquist at low sample rates. |
| Cutoff and resonance glide over 20 ms; coefficients are recalculated every 8 samples while gliding | Avoids zipper noise. Recalculating takes a `tan` and a `pow`; doing it every sample made a moving filter cost 3.5× as much (8.4 % of a core at 16 voices against 3.9 %), and no steps have been heard at 8 samples (0.17 ms). That's from listening, not a guarantee: a high resonance swept fast is where steps would show first, and an every-sample update is the fix if they ever do. The last sample of a glide always lands exactly on the new setting. |
| Key tracking 0–100 %, default 0 %, around middle C (MIDI 60) | At 100 % the cutoff moves an octave per octave played, so every note keeps the same tone; at 0 % (the old behaviour) it's fixed, so high notes sound duller. At middle C the cutoff is exactly the knob's setting. The tracked cutoff stays within 20 Hz – 20 kHz. At 0 % the cutoff is multiplied by exactly 1, so nothing changes. |
| A filter envelope per voice, its amount in octaves (−8 to +8, default 0) | Each note sweeps from its own key press, the reason for having a filter per voice. Octaves make the amount mean the same wherever the cutoff is: at the envelope's peak the cutoff is multiplied by 2^amount. At 0 the multiplier is exactly 1. |
| The filter envelope itself isn't smoothed; only its amount knob glides | Smoothing the envelope would blunt fast plucks, its main use. The cutoff follows it sample by sample, with coefficients recalculated every 8 samples, as for any moving cutoff (about 5 % of a core with all 16 voices sweeping). |
| The filter envelope's shape defaults to a pluck: no attack, 300 ms decay to 0 % sustain, 300 ms release | It has no effect until the amount is turned up, so the defaults are chosen to sound good the moment it is. |
| The per-block coefficient refresh only runs when nothing moves the cutoff | Refreshing at block starts while the envelope moved made the output depend on the host's block size (caught by the block-size test). While anything moves the cutoff, the every-8-samples updates handle it, at the same moments whatever the block size. Each note also restarts that 8-sample count, so a note never depends on what its voice played before. |
| Velocity → filter lowers softer notes' cutoff by `amount × (1 − velocity)` octaves (0 to 8, default 0) | The same idea as Velocity Sensitivity: the hardest hit always gets exactly what the knobs set, and turning it up only changes softer notes. Octaves keep it meaning the same wherever the cutoff is, as for the envelope amount. It's a straight line in octaves, so velocity 64 gets about half the amount. It's applied with key tracking, to the cutoff's target, so moving the knob mid-note still glides; at 0 it multiplies by exactly 1. It moves the cutoff itself rather than scaling the filter envelope's amount (another common choice), so it works with the envelope off. |
| One LFO shared by every note, running freely (not restarted by notes) | Like a hardware synth's single LFO: notes played together wobble together, in step. Restarting it on each note is optional step 23. It starts from the beginning of its cycle (rising from 0) when the host prepares the plug-in. |
| The LFO is worked out once per sample in `Synth`, for every voice, into a fixed 512-sample `Modulation` buffer | Working it out per voice would repeat the same sums 16 times. `Synth` overrides `renderVoices`, which JUCE calls between MIDI events, and splits longer stretches into pieces of up to 512 samples, so nothing allocates whatever the block size. It can't split the block before JUCE's MIDI handling instead, because `Synthesiser` handles every MIDI event past the end of a range it's given straight away, which would move later notes early. The LFO moves on one sample at a time in order, so the output doesn't depend on the block size (checked with blocks of 1, 7, 512 and 2048). |
| The LFO works out its shapes itself (`Lfo`), not from the notes' wave tables | Every destination's range assumes the LFO swings exactly from −1 to 1, so depth means the same for every shape. The notes' tables are loudness-matched, which scales the triangle and saw to ±1.22 and the square to ±0.71; read by the LFO, a saw would push tremolo below silence. The shapes are simple enough to compute per sample: the sine with `std::sin`, the others from straight lines. |
| LFO Shape: the first four waveforms' names and order, in a list of its own (default Sine) | The same four shapes as the oscillator, so the same names; saved by position, so new shapes go at the end. The list is separate from Waveform's (since step 28a) because wavetables join Waveform's and can't be LFO shapes: the LFO works its shapes out itself. Sine, the LFO's only shape before. A change crossfades over 10 ms, counted in whole samples, like a change of waveform. |
| The saw's and square's jumps are straight 5 ms ramps, whatever the rate | An instant jump clicks on the volume or the cutoff. 5 ms is the shortest release, the slope the amp envelope already treats as click-free. The square is otherwise flat at exactly ±1 (a trapezoid); the saw rises in a straight line from −1 to 1 between its drops, so to fit the drop in, its ramp is steeper by 2π / (2π − jump): 0.5 % at 1 Hz, 11 % at 20 Hz. The sine and triangle have no jumps and stay exact. |
| Not band-limited LFO tables (tried in step 21b) | Band-limiting softens a jump by keeping its harmonics below about 150 Hz, which at 5–20 Hz leaves only a handful, too few for corners: measured, a tapered (Fejér) triangle swung only to 0.88 at 5 Hz and 0.68 at 20 Hz. Cutting the harmonics off sharply instead rings past ±1 (to 1.18), and a gentler taper (Lanczos), scaled to peak at 1, left the square's top about 2 % low. Ramping only the jumps has none of these problems, and its edges are the same length at every rate rather than varying 2× between octave levels. |
| LFO Depth is a percentage (0–100 %, default 0), not semitones | Step 20 sends the same depth to volume or the cutoff, and a saved value can't be reinterpreted later without converting old projects. For pitch, 100 % is ±1 octave. |
| Pitch depth is 12 × depth² semitones | Squared, so the knob's travel goes to the small depths vibrato uses: 20 % is about ±½ semitone. At 0 % it's exactly 0, so the pitch is multiplied by exactly 1. |
| LFO Rate 0.05–20 Hz, knob centre 1 Hz, default 5 Hz | Slow sweeps to fast wobbles, skewed so equal turns are equal steps in speed. 5 Hz is a typical vibrato, ready for when the depth is turned up. Rate changes need no glide: the LFO's phase carries on smoothly at the new speed. |
| LFO Depth glides over 20 ms | So turning it up during a note doesn't jump the pitch. |
| The mod wheel adds to LFO Depth: knob + amount × wheel, at most 100 % (amount 0–100 %, default 0) | The usual arrangement: the knob sets a base, often none, and the wheel brings in more while playing. Default 0 % because older projects may hold mod wheel moves that used to do nothing; at 0 the depth is exactly the knob's. The wheel goes through the same 20 ms glide as the knob, so its 128 coarse steps don't zipper, and it takes effect from its own sample, since JUCE splits the block at each MIDI event. |
| One mod wheel for the whole synth, from any MIDI channel; "reset all controllers" (121) puts it down; its fine half (controller 33) is ignored | There's one LFO, so one wheel position. 128 steps, glided, are smooth enough for depth. The wheel keeps its position across notes and when the host prepares the plug-in, as a real wheel does. |
| LFO Retrigger (off by default) restarts the one shared LFO on every note-on | The classic "key sync" of a synth with one LFO, and it keeps the shared design: per-voice LFOs would mean working out the LFO 16 times over. Off by default, the free-running LFO from before. With one LFO, a new note restarts it for notes already held too, as on such synths. The restart happens on the note's own sample (JUCE splits the block at each MIDI event), so it doesn't depend on block size. |
| A restart fades over 5 ms from the wave carrying on where it was, the same fade a shape change uses | Restarting at once would jump the LFO, and the notes already sounding with it: a click on tremolo or the cutoff. `Lfo` keeps the outgoing wave (its shape and phase) running for the fade. A restart or shape change on the same sample a fade began keeps fading from the same outgoing wave and starts the fade over, so a chord, whose notes all restart the LFO on one sample, fades once (caught by a test that broke this). One part-way through a fade (a strum's notes a few milliseconds apart) fades instead from the value the LFO last gave, held steady: going back to the first fade's outgoing wave would jump the LFO back towards where it started (found by a code review; a test measured jumps of 0.47). |
| LFO Sync: a switch (off by default) and a Sync Rate choice, rather than one knob that changes units | One control whose meaning switches between Hz and note lengths can't be shown sensibly in a DAW's generic controls. Off, the rate is LFO Rate as before; on, LFO Rate is ignored. |
| Sync rates are note lengths in quarter notes, 4/1 down to 1/32 with dotted and triplet lengths (default 1/4) | The usual list, slowest first. Note lengths rather than bars, as most synths do, so they don't depend on the time signature. One cycle lasts the length at the tempo: rate = BPM / 60 / quarter notes. |
| While the host plays, a synced LFO follows the song's position, a cycle counted from the song's start | So it lines up with the beat the same way on every playback and export, which also makes the free-running limitation go away when synced. The processor reads the host's play head at the start of each block and gives it to the synth. While stopped (or without a song position) it runs freely at the tempo. |
| It's moved only when it has drifted more than 1/10,000 of a cycle from the song, then over a 5 ms fade | Advancing sample by sample at the tempo keeps it with the song in ordinary playback, so nothing moves it, and the output doesn't depend on block size (checked with blocks of 1, 7, 512 and 2048 while playing). Loops, jumps and starting playback move it, fading like a restart, as a jump would click. |
| With Retrigger on too, notes restart the LFO, and the song doesn't place it | The two can't both decide where the LFO is. "Key sync plus a synced rate" is the usual meaning when both are on. |
| No tempo from the host: 120 BPM, or the last tempo it gave | 120 BPM is the common default. Most hosts always give a tempo. |
| A synced LFO runs no faster than 50 Hz | So its 5 ms ramps always fit in its cycle. Only 1/32 above 375 BPM reaches it; capping at the Rate knob's 20 Hz would break sync at ordinary fast tempos (1/32 at 160 BPM is 21 Hz). |
| LFO destinations: pitch (default), volume, filter cutoff, wavetable position | Pitch is the default so projects from step 19 keep their vibrato. Each voice multiplies its pitch and level by the LFO's `Modulation`, adds its octaves to the cutoff and its offset to Position; for the destinations not chosen those are exactly 1, 1, 0 and 0, so nothing changes. |
| A Position envelope of its own, per voice, its amount from −100 to +100 % of the table (default 0) | So the filter and Position can move separately: sharing the filter envelope would save five knobs, but tie the two together for good, as IDs can't be renamed later. A share of the table means the same on every wavetable. At 0 it adds exactly 0. The shape defaults to the filter envelope's pluck, as it has no effect until the amount is turned up, and only the amount glides, so fast sweeps stay sharp. It only shapes Position: the amp envelope alone decides when a note ends. |
| Position is the knob, plus the envelope, plus the LFO, limited once | So equal and opposite pushes cancel at the extremes, unlike the cutoff, which is limited twice. |
| Four rows of groups, 902 × 608 at 100 %: Global, Oscillator and Amp Envelope; the Position and Filter Envelopes; Filter; LFO | The Position Envelope first went beside Oscillator on the top row, keeping three rows, but that took the window to 1322 pixels wide, which was too wide in use. A fourth row is 140 pixels taller and much narrower. Position Envelope sits under Oscillator, what it moves, and Filter Envelope right above Filter. |
| The Position LFO moves it by depth × the LFO, in a straight line, around the knob, and stops at the table's ends | At 100 % it can cross the whole knob either way, so the knob sets where it sweeps and depth how far: knob 50 % and depth 50 % covers the whole table exactly. Not squared as for pitch, as table sweeps want big moves, like the cutoff. Past an end it holds still rather than wrapping or folding back, as a knob would. On a waveform of one frame it has nothing to move. |
| Tremolo swings the level between full and 1 − depth | The classic tremolo, in plain gain. It only ever turns the level down, so it can't push the output past full scale, and at 100 % it reaches silence. It's applied per voice rather than to the synth's output, so a stolen note's fade keeps the level it was at. |
| The cutoff LFO moves it 4 × depth octaves either way, linearly | A filter wobble wants big sweeps, so unlike pitch it isn't squared: 50 % is ±2 octaves. It adds to the filter envelope's octaves, and the total is kept within 20 Hz – 20 kHz. Like the envelope, it does nothing while the filter is off. |
| A destination change crossfades over 10 ms, with a smoothed amount per destination | Switching from vibrato to tremolo at the bottom of the LFO's cycle would drop the note to silence in one sample. As with the filter type, each destination's amount glides between 0 and 1, so no second LFO is needed. |
| The voice notices, on the exact sample, when the LFO stops moving the cutoff | While the cutoff moves, coefficients update every 8 samples. When the LFO's depth glides to 0 partway through a block, the last update would otherwise wait for the next block's refresh, and block boundaries depend on the host. So `Modulation` says per sample whether the LFO moves the cutoff, and the voice updates once more on the sample it stops (caught by a block-size test that stops it mid-block). |

## Known limitations

- **A voice stolen five times within 5 ms** cuts its quietest fading note: each voice keeps four. That needs six notes on one voice in 5 ms with every voice busy.
- **A fading note keeps its last pitch, level and Position**, bend and the LFO included, and its filter as it was. Pitch bends and the LFO's movement during its 5 ms fade don't reach it, which is too short to hear.
- **The LFO moves one destination at a time.** Vibrato and a filter wobble together need a second LFO or a modulation matrix (optional step 33).
- **Fast saws and squares are a little rounder than ideal.** Their 5 ms ramps take a fifth of a 20 Hz cycle, so the square is visibly trapezoidal and the saw's rise is up to 11 % steeper. The ramps' corners are sharp: no click, but some synths round them too. Only the saw rising and the square starting high are offered; a falling saw (ramp down) would be a new shape at the end of the list.
- **Running freely, the LFO's position depends on when the host prepared the plug-in**, not on the song's position. The same note can catch the vibrato at a different point each time the song plays. LFO Retrigger makes each note repeatable, and LFO Sync, while the song plays, makes the LFO follow the song.
- **A tempo change takes effect at the next block.** The host gives the tempo once per block, so during a tempo ramp a synced LFO lags by up to a block, then the song lock nudges it back (with a fade) once it has drifted far enough.
- **One LFO for all notes.** With retrigger on, a new note restarts the vibrato of notes already held. Per-voice LFOs would let each note keep its own, at 16 times the work.
- **The envelope's stages are straight lines.** Many synths curve them (exponential decay and release), which can sound more natural. Easy to add later as an option, without breaking projects.
- **Brightness steps when bending saw or square** across a band-limiting level boundary. Crossfading between neighbouring levels would fix it if it's audible.
- **A second waveform change within 10 ms jumps.** A change crossfades from the old waveform over 10 ms; another change before that finishes starts a new crossfade from the waveform being faded *in*, dropping the blend that was playing. Only fast automation of Waveform does that. (Found by a code review and left: an LFO-style fix would hold an audio-rate wave still, which is worse.)
- **The cutoff is limited to 20 Hz–20 kHz twice**: once after key tracking and velocity, then again after the filter envelope and LFO. So at the extremes, equal and opposite offsets don't always cancel (a large negative velocity amount pinned at 20 Hz, then a positive envelope moving up from 20 Hz rather than from where it would have been). Combining every offset in octaves and limiting once would be cleaner, but it would change existing presets' sound at the extremes. (Found by a code review.)
- **High resonance can get loud.** A +20 dB peak on a strong harmonic can push the output past full scale, where the output guard clips it. Real synths often compensate by lowering the level as resonance rises; worth listening for.
- **The filter is perfectly clean.** Analog filters, and many digital ones, saturate (distort gently) when driven hard, which many people like. Ours doesn't, which also keeps it linear.
- **Key tracking follows the note played, not pitch bends.** A note bent up an octave keeps the cutoff of the key that was pressed. Some synths track the bent pitch; that could be added later.
- **Velocity only darkens.** Filter Velocity can lower softer notes' cutoff but not raise it, and it doesn't change how far the filter envelope sweeps. Scaling the envelope amount by velocity is a common alternative that could be added as its own parameter.
- **The preset menu doesn't show when you've changed a preset's settings.** It keeps showing the preset's name; save to keep the changes.
- **A chord of saws can snap now and then.** A saw drops sharply once a cycle, and as a chord's saws drift past each other, now and then several drop on the same instant: one big, sharp drop, heard as a click. Chopping the chord with a full-depth square LFO (Chopped Chords) makes it easy to hear, as each chop is heard on its own. Measured on a reported case (a Gm13 chord strummed in sixteenths at 92 BPM, rendered with `--render`): about three saws dropping together at 1.676 s, a drop of 0.72 in a quarter of a millisecond, its burst of high frequencies 1.87× a typical chop's. A lower filter cutoff didn't help (it dulls everything alike: 1.67× to 1.91×); a triangle removes it (1.00×) but sounds much softer. Starting each note at a random point in its wave was tried (step 26b) and dropped: it only moved the snap (to 1.973 s, 1.84×), since saws line up by chance whatever their starting points. It's what saw chords do; most noticeable when gated hard.
- **Adding a choice moves an old automation lane.** Projects store a choice by position, so they load correctly, but a host's automation lane stores it as 0 to 1 across the whole list: the wavetables moved Saw from 2/3 to 2/12. A lane already drawn on Waveform can land on a different choice, and likewise on LFO Destination now it has Position. The alternative, fixing the list's length in advance, would show empty choices in the menu.
- **A moving pitch costs more than a held one.** The level has to be found again on every sample during a bend or vibrato: 16 saws take about 3.5–3.9 % of a core, up from about 3.1 % before step 28b. Why it rose isn't fully understood; making the search's limit inline didn't help.
- **The first copy of the plug-in takes longer to load:** about 0.4 s in Release while it builds the tables, and again if every copy is removed and one is added later.
- **Wavetables made of high harmonics get quieter on high notes**, as those harmonics pass Nyquist. At C7 and Position 100 %, Formant is 13 dB below the sine, FM Hollow 9 dB and Wavefold 3 dB. Above that, Harmonics also fades. Rescaling each note to keep its level was left out: it would make a note's loudness jump as a bend crossed a level boundary.
- **The wavetable display shows one note.** With several playing, each with its envelope at a different place, it follows the newest. It updates at most 30 times a second, so a fast LFO looks slower or steppier than it sounds.
- **An imported wavetable makes projects and presets bigger:** up to about 2.8 MB each, and hosts that save a project's state often (for undo, say) save that each time.
- **Frame length is assumed to be 2048.** A file of another frame length that happens to be a multiple of 2048 samples (64 frames of 256, say) loads as the wrong frames, with no way to tell. Serum's files say their frame length in a `clm` chunk, which isn't read.
- **A replaced table stays in memory while anything might read it:** until every note that played it has ended, and, with a host that's stopped processing, until audio resumes (so the audio thread can move on to the newer one) or the plug-in is removed.
- **Not yet checked by tools:** Steinberg's VST3 validator, and building on macOS or Linux. pluginval is run by hand, not from CTest.

## Working on it

- **Tests:** `cmake --build build --config Release --target Tests`, then `ctest --test-dir build -C Release --output-on-failure`.
- **Sound unchanged?** Record before a change and compare after; see the README. The compare exits with a failure on any difference, or on one beyond a tolerance given in dBFS (`--compare build/reference.bin -120`).
- **Speed:** `Tests --benchmark` (Release build). Run it before and after anything that adds work per voice or per sample. **The numbers drift with how busy the computer is** (by 15–25 % between sessions), so to judge a change, benchmark the previous commit at the same time: `git stash`, rebuild, benchmark, `git stash pop`, rebuild, benchmark. Baseline on the development machine after step 26, as a share of one CPU core: no notes 0.3 %; 16 sine or saw notes about 3.3 %, with or without vibrato or the pitch wheel moving; 16 saw notes through a moving filter (its cutoff, envelope or the LFO moving it) about 6 %. The filter costs nothing while it's off.
- **A new parameter** also goes at the end of `laterParameterIds` in `tests/ParameterTests.cpp`; a test fails until it's there. That list is what the "older project" test removes from a saved state, and every parameter's save and reload is then checked automatically.
- **Prove a new test can fail:** break the code it protects on purpose, confirm the test fails, then restore the file and confirm it's byte-for-byte identical.
- **pluginval** at strictness 10 on both Release and Debug builds, since Debug turns on JUCE's internal checks.
- **Commits** are scanned for secrets by the gitleaks hook in `.githooks`.
- **README and docs** have no tests: after editing them, read the result.
